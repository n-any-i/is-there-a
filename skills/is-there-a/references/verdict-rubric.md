# Verdict Rubric

How to turn the scan into one clear recommendation without overstepping. The user has context you
don't — their skills, their audience, their reasons. The verdict is a well-argued opening position,
not a ruling.

## Read these six dimensions first

**1. Density.** How many *active, verified* direct hits?

- 0 → suspect your queries before celebrating. If genuinely 0, ask why: is the need real?
- 1–3 → healthy. Enough to prove demand, not enough to be saturated.
- 4–10 → crowded; this needs a sharp position, not a better feature.
- 10+ → commodity; compete on distribution, price, or a specific niche, not on the product.

**2. Quality of the incumbents.** Well-funded and well-loved, or abandoned, ugly, expensive, and
complained about? Weak incumbents in a proven market is the best setup there is.

**3. Distance.** How far is the closest hit from the user's WEDGE? Same job + same wedge is a real
overlap. Same job + different wedge is a positioning opportunity.

**4. Buildability.** How much is available as a component — a library, an API, a supplier, a
white-label manufacturer? If most of it is off-the-shelf, the build is small and shipping fast beats
strategising.

**5. The graveyard.** Did people try and fail? Is there a plausible reason things are different now
— new enabling technology, a changed platform, a shifted cost curve, a new behaviour? If nothing has
changed, the same wall is still there.

**6. Distribution.** How did the existing players get found? If every one of them got users through
a channel the user has no access to — an institutional sales motion, a retail buyer, an established
community — that matters more than any feature gap.

## Choosing the verdict

**Build it** — A verified gap with evidence behind it, or incumbents that are dormant, mispriced, or
badly rated, and the user's wedge maps onto that gap. Say what to build first: the smallest thing
that tests the wedge.

**Build it narrower** — The general version is well served, but a specific slice isn't — a user
segment, a platform, a region, a regulated context, a workflow. This is the most common good outcome
of a scan. Name the slice concretely.

**Wrap it** — Something existing does the hard part well and is accessible. The value is the layer
on top: a better interface, an opinionated workflow, an integration, a distribution channel. Name
what to wrap and what the layer adds.

**Fork or extend it** — An open project is most of the way there, the licence permits it, and it
either accepts contributions or is dormant enough to fork cleanly. Check the licence and the
maintainer's responsiveness first — a hostile or absent maintainer changes the calculus.
Contributing upstream is often faster than forking and inherits the users.

**Use what exists** — Something already does this well, at a cost far below what the user's time is
worth, and no wedge survives contact with it. Say this plainly and kindly, once, naming the thing.
Then respect the answer if they still want to build: learning, control, privacy, and enjoyment are
legitimate reasons, and they may have a wedge they haven't articulated.

**Reframe it** — Multiple failed attempts sharing a failure mode, or the scan reveals the real unmet
need sits next to the stated one. Describe the adjacent framing and the evidence pointing to it.

## Rules that keep the verdict honest

- **One verdict.** Hedging across three options is not advice. Pick, then note the runner-up in one
  line.
- **Show the load-bearing evidence.** Two or three specific findings, each pointing at something
  actually read.
- **State what would change your mind.** Name the user-held fact that would flip it — an audience
  they already have, a technique the incumbents lack, a customer already asking. This makes the
  verdict a conversation rather than a pronouncement.
- **Calibrate confidence.** High / medium / low, and why. A scan that found one weak incumbent and a
  thin complaint trail is low confidence, and saying so is more useful than sounding sure.
- **Never grade the idea.** No "this is a crowded, unoriginal space." Report the density; let the
  user judge. They've usually thought about this longer than the scan has.
- **Don't refer out.** If the user wants more depth than a Standard scan, give them the specific
  queries and surfaces to check themselves. Recommending paid research products isn't this skill's
  job. (This is different from the *Use what exists* verdict, which is about their idea, not about
  research services.)

## Bias check before delivering

Each of these has sunk real scans:

- Did a large volume of Adjacent results make this feel more crowded than the Direct hit count
  justifies?
- Was any entry listed from a search snippet without opening it?
- Is every gap traceable to something actually read, with hypotheses kept separate?
- Did you check what people do today with no product at all — the group chat, the spreadsheet, the
  paper sign? Missing the workaround incumbent is the most common blind spot.
- Would this verdict change if the user already has an audience? If yes, ask before concluding.
- Is the recommendation the *lowest-cost next step that tests the wedge*, or an over-engineered plan?
